# Lung cancer

Lung cancer is cancer found in the lungs. It's one of the most common types of cancer in the UK.

## What is lung cancer?

- Lung cancer is cancer that's found in one or both of your lungs.
- The lungs are an organ in your body that help you breathe.
- How serious lung cancer is depends on how big the cancer is, if the cancer has spread, and your general health.
- Lung cancer is one of the most common types of cancer in the UK.
- Lung cancer screening can pick up lung cancer early, which may mean it's easier to treat.

## Symptoms of lung cancer

### Main symptoms of lung cancer

Common symptoms of lung cancer may include:

- a cough that is not going away
- shortness of breath
- coughing up blood
- chest or shoulder pain
- often having chest infections, or having a chest infection that is not getting better
- losing weight without trying
- not feeling hungry (loss of appetite)
- feeling tired all the time

Less common symptoms of lung cancer may include:

- swelling of your face or neck
- difficulty swallowing
- a hoarse voice that does not go away
- changes in the way your fingers look (clubbing) – your fingertips may look wider than usual or curve downwards.

You may not notice any symptoms in the early stages of lung cancer.

### When and where to get medical help

If you have any symptoms of lung cancer, it's important to get checked.

#### Non-urgent advice: See a GP if:

- you've had a cough for longer than 3 weeks
- you have any other symptoms of lung cancer

#### Urgent advice: Ask for an urgent GP appointment or get help from NHS 111 if:

- you've coughed up blood or noticed blood on a tissue you've coughed into

You can call 111 or get help from 111 online.

#### Immediate action required: Call 999 or go to A&E if:

- you're struggling to breathe – you are choking, gasping and unable to speak
- you have pain in your chest or upper back

Do not drive to A&E. Ask someone to drive you or call 999 and ask for an ambulance.

Bring any medicines you take with you.

#### Important

Some of these symptoms are very common and can be caused by other conditions.

Having the symptoms does not definitely mean you have lung cancer, but it's important to get checked by a GP.

If your symptoms are caused by cancer, finding it early may mean it's easier to treat.

### What happens at your GP appointment

The doctor will ask you more about your symptoms, general health, medical history, and if anyone in your family has had lung cancer.

They may ask to listen to your chest to check if your breathing sounds normal. You can ask for someone else to be in the room with you when this happens.

You may also be asked to have a blood test, or an X-ray of your chest.

#### Referral to a specialist

You may get an urgent referral for more tests or to see a specialist in hospital if the GP thinks you have symptoms that could be cancer. This does not definitely mean you have cancer.

## Causes of lung cancer

### Who is more likely to get lung cancer

It's not always known what causes lung cancer, but it can be caused by lifestyle, genetic changes, and environmental factors.

Having certain health conditions can also make you more likely to get lung cancer.

You may be more likely to get lung cancer if:

- you smoke
- you've often breathed in other peoples' smoke over a long period of time (passive smoking)
- you have chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD)
- a close relative has had lung cancer
- you work or used to work in the building industry and may have breathed in asbestos fibres over a long period of time
- you've breathed in air pollution over a long period of time
- you've breathed in radon over a long period of time – radon is a natural gas that's sometimes found in high levels inside buildings in some areas of the UK

#### Find out more

- Risks and causes of lung cancer (Cancer Research UK)
- UK radon information (GOV.UK)

### How to lower your risk of lung cancer

#### Do

- quit smoking
- stay active and exercise regularly
- eat a healthy diet including at least 5 portions of fruit and vegetables a day

It's important to get any symptoms of lung cancer checked by a GP.

## Tests and next steps for lung cancer

### Main tests for lung cancer

If a GP refers you to a specialist because they think you could have lung cancer, you'll have tests to check for cancer.

The main tests for lung cancer are:

- a CT scan
- a bronchoscopy, where a thin, flexible tube with a small camera on the end is passed down your throat and into your lungs to look inside

#### Find out more

- Tests for lung cancer (Cancer Research UK)

### Getting your results

You may get some results on the same day you have your tests, or after a few days or weeks.

You can call the hospital or your GP if you're worried and they may be able to update you if your results are ready.

#### If you're told you have lung cancer

Being told you have lung cancer can feel overwhelming. You may be feeling anxious about what will happen.

You'll be helped through your diagnosis and treatment by a team of specialists.

This team will include a clinical nurse specialist who will be your main point of contact during and after your treatment. You can ask them any questions you may have.

##### Get support

Macmillan Cancer Support has a free helpline that's open every day from 8am to 8pm.

They're there to listen if you have anything you want to talk about.

Call 0808 808 00 00.

### Next steps

If you've been told you have lung cancer, you may need more tests which can include:

- an MRI scan
- a PET scan
- a biopsy
- blood tests
- genetic testing

The results of these tests can show what type of cancer you have, how far the cancer has spread, and if the cancer has been caused by certain changes in your genes.

This will help your specialist team decide what treatment you need.

## Treatment for lung cancer

### Main treatments for lung cancer

The treatment you'll have for lung cancer depends on:

- the size of the cancer and where it is
- the type of lung cancer you have
- if it has spread to other parts of your body
- if the cancer has certain genetic changes
- your general health
- which treatment options you decide are right for you

You may be offered a combination of treatments including surgery, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and targeted medicines.

Your specialist treatment team will:

- explain the treatments, the benefits and side effects
- work with you to make a treatment plan that's best for you
- help you to manage the side effects of treatment

If you have any questions or worries, you can talk to your specialist team.

### Surgery

You may have surgery to remove cancer from your lung. Part or all of your lung may also need to be removed.

You'll be supported through surgery and recovery by your specialist treatment team.

#### Find out more

- Types of surgery for lung cancer (Cancer Research UK)

### Chemotherapy

Chemotherapy is medicine that kills cancer cells.

You may have chemotherapy for lung cancer:

- if you're unable to have surgery
- if cancer has spread to other parts of your body
- before surgery to try to make your tumour smaller
- after surgery to stop the cancer coming back

#### Find out more

- Chemotherapy for lung cancer (Cancer Research UK)

### Radiotherapy

Radiotherapy uses radiation to kill cancer cells. A machine is used to target beams of radiation directly at tumours.

You may have radiotherapy for lung cancer:

- if you're unable to have surgery
- after surgery to stop the cancer coming back
- if the cancer has spread to other parts of your body

Find out more

- Radiotherapy for lung cancer (Cancer Research UK)

### Targeted medicines and immunotherapy

Targeted medicines kill cancer cells or stop them growing as quickly.

Immunotherapy is where medicines are used to help your immune system kill cancer.

Targeted medicines or immunotherapy are sometimes used to treat advanced lung cancer that has spread to other parts of the body.

#### Find out more

- Targeted and immunotherapy treatment for lung cancer (Cancer Research UK)

### What happens if you've been told your cancer cannot be cured

If you've been diagnosed with advanced lung cancer, it may be hard to treat and not possible to cure.

The aim of treatment will be to slow down the growth and spread of the cancer, to help with the symptoms, and help you live longer.

Finding out cancer cannot be cured can be very hard news to take in.

You'll be referred to a team of doctors and nurses called a symptom control team or palliative care team.

They will help you to manage your symptoms and make you feel more comfortable.

The palliative care team can also help you and your loved ones get any other support you need.
